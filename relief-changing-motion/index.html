Learn

What can a force do to motion?

Read everything on this page carefully. You will need all of it for the worksheet.

Five ways a force changes motion (plus one more)

Start

A still object starts moving. Kicking a ball that was sitting on the grass.

Stop

A moving object comes to rest. A goalie catching the ball.

Speed up

A moving object goes faster. A skydiver just after jumping.

Slow down

A moving object goes slower. A bike rolling up a hill.

Change direction

Turns, bounces or curves. A ball bouncing off a wall.

Change shape

Squashes, stretches or bends. This is not a change in motion, but it is still something a force can do.

Every change in motion needs an UNBALANCED force.

Balanced forces: no change

The forces in opposite directions are the same size, so they cancel out. The net force is 0 N.

  • A still object stays still.
  • A moving object keeps going at the same speed in the same direction.

Watch out: something can be moving and still have balanced forces. A car cruising at a steady 60 km/h in a straight line has balanced forces.

Unbalanced forces: motion changes

One force is bigger than the force pushing the other way. There is a net force, and the object's motion changes.

  • Net force the same way it is moving: it speeds up.
  • Net force the opposite way to its motion: it slows down (and may stop).
  • Net force sideways to its motion: it changes direction.

Working out the net force

When forces act in opposite directions, take the smaller one away from the bigger one. The net force points the same way as the bigger force.

Push 50 N right, friction 20 N left
50 − 20 = 30 N to the right

If the forces are equal, the net force is 0 N and they are balanced.

Tricky one: changing direction

A car drives around a roundabout at a steady 30 km/h. Its speed does not change, so are the forces balanced?

No. Its direction keeps changing, and changing direction counts as changing motion. Friction from the tyres pushes the car towards the centre of the roundabout. That sideways force is unbalanced.

Short response

Explain what happens to a hockey puck after it is hit across the ice, and why it eventually stops.

Try to use all four:

Model answer: When the stick hits the puck there is a large unbalanced force, so the puck starts moving fast. After it leaves the stick, the only forces acting along the ice are friction from the ice and air resistance. These are small, but they are unbalanced and act backwards, against the motion. The net force is backwards, so the puck slowly slows down until it stops.

Compare: did you say what happens when it is hit, what force acts after, which way the net force points, and why that makes it stop? Add anything you missed to your answer.
